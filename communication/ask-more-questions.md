# Ask More Questions

- A small misunderstanding early can turn into a big misunderstanding later, because all the things you misinterpret based on the first misunderstanding will become misunderstandings in their own right, and so on
- Sometimes people don’t ask questions because they’re not really trying to understand. They’re happy to just trust that the person they’re talking to knows what’s going on
    - This especially happens when that person is a well-respected or more senior engineer
- However, once you have skin in the game, that all changes
    - When you’re in a position where you are the one responsible for success or failure of a project you will find yourself wanting to ask many questions.
    - Success or failure here means where you are going to have to go away after the conversation and take a bunch of concrete actions

## Where communication gets lossy
Your job is to keep the parts people need to act:

1. Vantage point

E.g. The executive who has spent weeks debating a strategy sees something different from the manager encountering it for the first time.

2. Relay hops

E.g. Inside a company, intent travels from CEO to VP to director to manager to engineer. Each layer translates the message into its own language and priorities.

3. The pointer

E.g. “That thing.” “There.” These words only resolve if the listener shares your picture, and every organization runs on them. Think of “the priority,” “the big customer,” “fix onboarding,” and “move faster.” They sound specific to the person saying them.

Gaps
1. The knowledge gap is the difference between what we’d like to know and what we know
2. The effects gap is the difference between what we expect our actions to achieve and what they produce
3. The alignment gap is the distance between what we want people to do and what they do

## Checking what communication survived

The Army's backbrief: the subordinate explains what they’re going to do, and the commander hears it before anyone moves.

Leaders explain the intent. Teams explain what they understood and how they plan to act. Then both sides adjust. Sometimes the plan changes, and sometimes the leader discovers the intent wasn’t as clear or as sensible as it sounded.

A few habits make that kind of correction more likely:

1. Name the thing: replace “the priority” with the name of the project and “the big customer” with the company. Don’t make people guess which part of your mental picture you’re pointing at.

2. Send the why with the what: explain what you’re trying to achieve, and give people enough context to recognize success when they see it.

3. Count the hops: for anything that matters, skip a level or two and listen. Ask an engineer, not a VP, what the company is trying to do this quarter. Don’t ask whether the strategy is clear. Ask what they’re doing because of it.

4. Rank, and say what loses: priorities only become useful when they force a choice. If speed matters more than completeness, say so. If customer trust beats this quarter’s revenue, say that too. People need to know what they’re allowed to sacrifice.

## Call to Action
- You should all be asking way more questions
- You should all be trying harder to actually understand what you’re hearing
- Don’t trust that the person (or AI model) you’re talking to knows what they’re doing, even if you think they’re smarter than you
- Nobody understands complex software products
