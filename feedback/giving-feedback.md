# Giving Feedback

## Why give feedback

A feedback conversation is a sales conversation. You are pitching the person on why and how to change — which means empathizing with what matters to them, then crafting your message around that.

Other reasons:

- **Builds trust.** It shows your manager or report that you don't only notice the negative. Fight for your team.
- **Strength-based development.** Help people know their strengths and weaknesses, then use their strengths to navigate around weaknesses. Fixing a weakness often leads to mediocre results; doubling down on a strength can lead to exceptional ones.
- **Cultural change.** Pointing out the exact behavior you want to see accelerates norm-setting.
- **Self-inspection.** A great exercise in clarifying what you actually want from your team.

## Before you speak

Ask yourself: *Is what I'm about to say strategy or self-expression?*

If it's self-expression — frustration, venting, scoring a point — stop. If it's strategy — something that will genuinely help the person — continue.

## What to include

Be specific about what you want the person to do going forward, and pitch why the new behavior benefits them:

- How does this make them more effective?
- How does this help them work better with the people around them?
- How does this get them closer to their goals?
- How is this a skill they can apply now and in all future roles?

## Cautions

- Never give generic feedback — always be specific
- Don't ask someone to change a behavior without creating opportunities for them to practice the new one

## SBI Framework (Situation, Behavior, Impact)

**Situation** — Avoid generalizations. Be concrete about what happened and when.

**Behavior** — What did the person do? What choices did they have? What effort did they take? Focus on actions, not characteristics.

**Impact** — What was the outcome of the behavior?

**Avoid the feedback sandwich** (praise → criticism → praise). The messages negate each other, and the person either leaves thinking things are fine or fixates only on the negative.

### Examples

🚫 *"You're a great developer who can solve every complex situation with an elegant, pragmatic approach."* — No situation, no concrete actions.

🚫 *"Thanks for taking care of the interview last week, you had some amazing questions."* — Concrete situation, but it's unclear what was good about those questions, and no impact.

🚫 *"I don't know what I would do without you, you are the person who holds this team together."* — Too generic; the person can't know what to repeat.

✅ *"It was a great idea to record a demo of the new feature you worked on. I used the video in the product review, and stakeholders immediately understood the UX problems we mentioned in the ticket's comments."*

✅ *"The new hire told me in our 1:1 how much he appreciated your guidance setting up his local dev environment. I liked that you were not stepping in and doing everything for him, but let him lead while being available whenever he was lost. Thank you for making sure the onboarding documentation is updated too."*

✅ *"Thanks for the stellar coordination during the Sunday incident: you mitigated the problem quickly by deploying a previous working build before the owner team was reached, and kept communicating with stakeholders so they were assured we had things under control even while the sites were down."*

## Language Techniques

### "Even more"

Assumes the person already does the thing — you're just asking them to do more of it. This avoids putting them in a binary "did or didn't do it" position.

🚫 "We might need to give guidance to new hires." (Binary — implies they didn't do it.)
✅ "We may need to give *even more* guidance to new hires." (Spectrum — assumes they already gave some.)

### "This is a great start" vs. "This looks great"

Use when you want to acknowledge effort but more can be done. Avoids sending mixed signals.

### "I noticed…"

Makes feedback more objective and concrete.

🚫 "You are long-winded."
✅ "I noticed in your response just now, it took a while to get to your main point. We've talked about being more concise, so I wanted to point this out as an example to keep an eye on."

### "At the same time"

Use when "but" feels too negative. Lets you name two competing realities without discrediting either.

🚫 "This idea works well for our current situation, but not for what we need going forward."
✅ "This idea works well for our current situation. At the same time, going forward we will deal with new variables A and B, which makes me think we may want to consider [alternative option] too."

### "I believe you were trying to X. This doesn't quite work because of Y. I recommend trying Z."

Use when you understand the recipient's intent and want to help them reach their goal better. Offering an alternative shows credibility as a partner.

🚫 "This email isn't compelling."
✅ "I believe you were trying to get the reader excited to take action, but the way the email is currently structured, it feels very logistics heavy. I recommend first selling them on why this matters — which you can do in a few lines — then share the logistics of what to do."

### "Already"

Use when you want to build on what the person is currently doing.

🚫 "You need to speak up."
✅ "You're already speaking up regularly in our team meetings, which is fantastic. Now, I'd love for you to speak up more in all-hands meetings to share what your team is working on with the broader company."

### Use yourself as an example

Talk about yourself so it doesn't seem like you're pointing fingers.

🚫 "You should try X."
✅ "I used to struggle with this, and when I tried X, it really helped."

### Diplomatic, curiosity-forward language

These phrases ease into a suggestion in a way that feels non-threatening:

- "We might want to try…"
- "I wonder if..."
- "Perhaps we could..."
- "What are your thoughts on..."
- "One approach might be..."

You position yourself as a helpful team member with the other person's best interest in mind.

## Bring Data and Examples

🚫 "We need to give more feedback during projects."
✅ "I noticed when we reviewed the design mockups weekly with the client, they loved the final result. For this project, early check-ins could help us make sure we're aligned before going too far down a certain direction and could prevent rework."

🚫 "The team needs clearer priorities."
✅ "The team made amazing progress when we all focused on the website update last month. It might help to have one or two clear priorities for the team this month that everyone can rally behind."

🚫 "We should spend more time training new people."
✅ "When we were able to dedicate that first week to training Steve, he got up to speed pretty quickly. The bit of upfront time seemed to have paid off, and taking a similar training approach for our next hire could help them ramp up just as fast. What do you think?"

Why this works:
- Ends with a clear, actionable suggestion
- Shows the upside of updating their approach
- Keeps it simple, logical, and positive
- The open-ended follow-up invites ownership
