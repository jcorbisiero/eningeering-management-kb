# Peer Feedback

## A key principle

People are expected to succeed despite imperfect coworkers. Imperfect coworker performance is a reality, not an excuse. One of the teachable moments in a peer feedback discussion is making this explicit.

## Set the expectation upfront

Tell your reports directly that you expect them to have opinions about their peers' performance:

> "We strongly value peer feedback and the ability to understand and evaluate peer performance. As we work together, I'll periodically ask you how your peers are doing and I'd like you to be able to have thoughtful conversations about it."

> "It's quite important that you have a mature opinion on how your cross-functional partners are doing. Please make sure that you're thinking about the different roles and responsibilities you share, how they are performing, and where you see opportunities for improvement."

## When they say someone is doing well

Dig into why — it's a teaching moment. Help the person understand:

- The difference between superhuman performance and expectations (people often glamorize someone who is simply doing a solid job)
- The ingredients of a strong performer's success (sometimes it's as simple as working really hard)
- The differences in leveling and experience (a person three levels above you doing their job well is expected, not exceptional)

## When they say someone is doing poorly

Also a teaching moment. Explore whether it's underperformance, the *perception* of underperformance, or a misunderstanding. Help them understand:

- What the role of that person actually is. Many critical perceptions come from not understanding what someone is supposed to do. (Your PM is not your project manager.)
- The differences in leveling and experience. Someone four levels below you may not realize what "good" looks like at a senior level.

## Use it as a continuous signal

Getting significant critical feedback from multiple people about a specific person is almost always a signal of underperformance. Collecting this continuously — not just at annual review cycles — is far more valuable.

Also: awareness of a peer's specific weaknesses lets teammates compensate. If Bob isn't great at meeting follow-ups, his manager can work on that — but someone who is great at it can help the team not fail in the meantime.
