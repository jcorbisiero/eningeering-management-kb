# Creating Opportunities

## Responsibility Is Taken Before It Is Given

* To get promoted as a software engineer in Big Tech, you generally need to show "ownership" of a product or area. This means being responsible for it and deciding what should happen over time.  
* Generate ideas and see which ones start receiving a simple "yes, go ahead." Over time, you should be setting the agenda: talking to users, understanding their problems, translating those into code changes and deciding what to improve next.  
* To be clear, this does not mean grabbing projects or stepping on other people to demonstrate ownership. If you exercise sound judgement within your existing scope, a reasonable TL should gradually trust you with more responsibility.  
* Formal responsibility is a trailing indicator, not a leading one. You'll find that people begin trusting and deferring to your judgement before the responsibility becomes explicit. Responsibility is taken in small increments before it is given in full.

## Observe, Assert, Validate Framework

* Sometimes asking your manager what they need help with is not the most effective way to ask how to help. Your manager needs to consider:  
  * "What are all the things I need help with? There are a lot of potential things."  
  * "What's the overlap of things I need help with, that this person might actually have the skills, judgment, and capacity to do?"  
  * "How long would it take to explain this to them? This might actually cost me more time to teach them how to do this."  
  * "How much do I believe in their ability to help?"  
  * "How much does this person already know? How much context do I need to get them up to speed?"  
  * "What's the risk if they mess something up?"  
* This does not mean do not ask to help but there is a better way to implement this  
* When applying the framework, keep in mind your goals, how it can help you get promoted, and if there is alignment or overlap with the company's goals  
* **Observing:** your manager might not be able to articulate what they need. When you observe, you notice what might be helpful for your manager, even if they haven't verbalized it. Look for clues, revealed preferences, implicit feedback, and what's unspoken. Observing is something you can do without anyone's permission. You can start doing it today.  
* **Asserting:** requires you to develop a point of view. You're asserting a potential problem and what you could do to solve it. When you assert, you can be wrong. If everyone keeps asking, "Well, what do you think?" There's no forward progress.  
* **Validating:** once you assert, you should validate whether you're going in the right direction

### Skeleton

* Observe: "I noticed X. This is important because…"  
* Assert: "Here's what I think we should do… / Here's what I can do…"  
* Validate: "What do you think? / How does that sound? / Am I missing anything?"

### Concrete example

* Observe: "I noticed in our Monday morning meetings, we've needed to pull the X data and it's a manual process. It requires about 2 hours of work each week, which adds up."  
* Assert: "I can create an automated table that tracks our weekly performance, so we can see trends more easily."  
* Validate: "This should take half a day to build and I'd like to take a stab at it. How does that sound?"

## Example as a Staff Engineer

* Don't act "strategically", but in your day to day, absorb the problems talked about  
* Overtime these problems can surface across many groups and can present an opportunity to fix something your leadership is not yet aware of  
* When something overlaps with my area, I start pulling on the thread. I might ask, "If X existed, would it solve your problem?" or point them at an existing feature in a product I own and ask how much of their use case it covers  
* Users often ask for a particular solution instead of explaining their root issue. Instead of taking their request at face value, keep digging until you understand what they are trying to accomplish and why existing products do not work for them  
* As a problem seems worth exploring become more active  
  * How it affects the team's day-to-day work  
  * Sit with them as they walk me through their workflows and the bugs they're investigating  
  * Try working through some of those bugs myself  
  * Seeing the problem firsthand makes it easier to separate what the team actually needs from the solution they asked for  
* Seek out people who see more of the organization than you do: those who own critical systems, work across several teams or have particularly deep insight into the work downstream of my team  
* Don't create something immediately, let these types of problems exist for a bit  
  * Waiting means the same problem might pop up independently in different teams, making it a higher priority to solve  
  * Problems that look different on the surface might turn out to have the same shape, so I can address several use cases in one shot  
  * Or you may learn painfully the requesting team didn't even care that much in the first place  
  * So make a mental note and revisit the problem if it comes up again. What matters is keeping unresolved problems around long enough for more evidence to accumulate.  
  * Waiting helps collect evidence  
* Simple example  
  * Owning a UI that now many different teams are asking for different aspects of what they want in their view  
  * Instead of implementing each request as it comes through, waited a bit to see a larger picture where teams just want customization so this lets you build something that solves multiple problems  
* **Building Trust and Stewardship**  
  * When you show genuine interest in someone's problem, ask useful questions or help solve it, they remember  
  * They start coming to me earlier and bring me into conversations with other people facing related issues  
  * That gives me a wider view of what is happening across the organization, making it easier to spot patterns and build things people actually need  
  * Solving one of those problems brings me into more conversations, and the loop continues
