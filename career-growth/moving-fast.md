# Moving Fast

Don't question why. Fast is better than slow. That's just how it is. Your job is to take everything you can already do and do it faster and still deliver outcomes.

## Suggestions

- If you're fast, you get data more quickly. That helps you make better decisions, sooner. It also means you learn faster, and over longer periods it means you learn more. Being fast also means you can try out multiple approaches to a problem and pick the best one.
- Don't delay
  - don't decide to tackle a big problem next week or next quarter
  - I think this is often about avoiding discomfort. Getting started is hard: you often don't know exactly what needs to be done, or where to begin. It's comforting to believe that waiting will make it easier, but in my experience it rarely does.
- Reclaim the small chunks
  - Some programmers have convinced themselves that they need long, uninterrupted work periods to get anything done. As I wrote in Getting things done (in small increments), I think this is more of a preference than a hard constraint. Most people could get better at this if they tried.
  - You can get a lot more done if you spend that time being productive rather than reading email or browsing Hacker News.
  - Concrete things that can help
    - Make progress every day
    - Always know what the next thing to do is \- having either a clear end goal (e.g., fix this bug, make this test green) or a concrete next step
    - Pay attention to feedback loops \- you need feedback loops faster given shorter work chunks
    - For bigger things, make a plan and keep a log \- take 10-15 minutes to write down a plan before I start the work
    - Block distractions
- Don't worry about looking dumb
  - Share your work early and often. But it's uncomfortable, so it's easy to put it off while telling yourself a story like "I have a high bar for quality."
  - If you're roughly 70% happy with a piece of writing you've produced, you should publish it. If you're 70% satisfied with a product you've created, launch it.
  - Ask your colleagues for advice. I've seen many developers who seem to think they're required to come up with everything themselves. But software development is a team sport, don't force yourself to go it alone.
- Pick your battles
  - If your PR reviewer wants you to change something, it's almost always faster to do what they're asking than to argue about it.
  - 95% of the time, the differences are so minor that it's barely worth discussing. Save your time and energy for the 5% that matter.
  - Don't rubber stamp everything; I often leave comments suggesting better names, or other ways to do things, but leave the final decision up to the author.
- Do not over engineer
  - If you try to go "above and beyond", you're almost always guessing about what someone else wants, or what the system will require in the future. And the more inexperienced you are, the greater the chance that your guess is wrong.
