# Managing Up

## What is it

The first rule of managing up: think from your manager's perspective.

Managing up is proactively working to understand, support, and guide your manager and higher-ups so that you can all succeed.

Your manager may have a broader view of the organization, different priorities, and constraints that you cannot see. At the same time, you have a much closer view of your team, your projects, and the problems your engineers are facing. Neither perspective is complete on its own without a mechanism for reconciliation.

Ask yourself: What behaviors would a report display that would make you have confidence in them and love working with them?

## Bridge the gap between your intent and others' perception of it

People often have great plans and good intentions. The problem is often that our intentions might not always be known, and our ways of seeing things and our actions might not always match others' perceptions of us. Your manager may see a problem but might not know why you're choosing not to solve it yet. They might not even know if you're aware of it or doing anything about it. You may have put off a much bigger fire, but your manager doesn't see that. The best way to get the support you need and to have space to do the work that serves you and the organization is by managing up.

The simplest way to describe managing up is to think of your actions as a brand. A brand is what you do, how you do it, and why you do it. The problem with creating a brand is that other people's perceptions of these 3 things matter more than your own perception.

## What every manager wants

- Someone good at their job
- Someone who they can trust and rely on
- Someone whose judgment they trust
- Someone who gives them less trouble
- Someone who will help them succeed and achieve their goals
- Someone they can connect and relate with.

And so on.

## Be good at your job and at what your manager expects you to do

- The first thing about managing up is understanding what your manager expects of you
- Ask questions to ensure your priorities align with what they want you to work on and to be good at those things.

## Make yourself reliable

Key takeaway: Don't wait, take action, make a plan, have a solution

- Every manager loves a report they can assign something to and never have to worry about it.
- Ways to make yourself reliable include doing those things and making it a habit to follow up with status or progress updates
- Proactively communicate where things are, what you're doing next, and the next step you're going to take.

## Overcommunicate

- An act of managing up never surprises your stakeholders meaning if things are going well, let them know and if it's not going well, let them know
- The element of surprise might jeopardize what you have been trying to put together
- Even if you can fix the problem, it's still a problem, and your managers deserve to know about it. By not surprising your boss, their confidence grows in you.

## Come forward with ideas, anticipate problems, and propose solutions

- Don't stop at sharing problems; also include your plan to resolve it and the help you need from others to solve it.

## Help keep your manager and stakeholders accountable.

More often than not, every meeting you have with a stakeholder or someone higher up will leave you with an item you either need to do or follow up on. Sometimes it's something you need to do or follow up on; other times, it's something they need to do or follow up on.

There are a few ways this can play out:

- Everyone forgets about it. Nothing happens.
- You do your part and move on. The work gets done, but you never close the loop with the person who asked for it.
- You close the loop. When you meet again, you recap what you discussed, what was agreed upon, what each person owned, and what has happened since.

The third option makes you look the most accountable.

What I recommend is that at the beginning of your next meeting, take a few minutes to recap the previous conversation:

- What you discussed
- What you all agreed on
- What did the stakeholder say they would do
- What you own and the progress you made
- What is still outstanding

This does two things:

- You establish yourself as someone who is accountable and follows through on commitment
- You create accountability for the other person

## Anti-patterns

- Many people don't manage up because they have a negative perception of managing up
- Some manage up in one area and leave out other areas, especially when it comes to creating awareness about what they personally did and why
- Some might even see it as bootlicking, or don't want to come off as bragging or self-promoting, which can be seen negatively in some cultures and contexts
